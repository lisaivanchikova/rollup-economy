Which industry to build an MSP in

Of 46 US industries, only six combine an enforced regulatory mandate with an open vendor layer — 41,851 firms in the 10–499 employee band. They form two clusters: healthcare around HIPAA (33,678 firms) and defence contracting around CMMC (8,173 firms, mandatory from 10 November 2026). In every other industry either the mandate carries no penalty, or the vendor layer is already taken.

Which raises the next question: the colour scale grades penalties rather than the existence of a rule. Why, and what fell out as a result, is on the next tab.

What the map deliberately omits. The cost of serving one seat. It is not observable in any public source, so it is absent from the axes and no estimate has been substituted for it. The vertical axis stands in for it indirectly: higher payroll means more seats per employee, and therefore a lower cost to serve each one.

And minimising cost to serve is probably the wrong target. A specialised MSP charges $250–350 per seat against $100–120 for a generalist, at a margin 23 points higher. The most expensive industries to serve are precisely those with a mandatory audit. Expensive to serve and expensive to sell are one property, not two. The target worth optimising is the widest gap between seat price and cost to serve.

A rule without a penalty is not a moat — half the industries fall out on this test

The regulatory moat is the only thing that holds a client and justifies a seat price above the market. But having a requirement and having consequences for breaching it are different things, and they diverge often.

Which raises the question that follows: in the six industries that form the answer, the vendor layer is open. Does that mean a service there can be grown into a product — and earn a software multiple instead of a services one?

An open vendor layer promises no product: in forty years no MSP has built a product for its own client industry

Thirty-odd companies checked against primary documents — registration statements and annual reports. The migrations that do exist fall into two types, and only one of them is available to an MSP.

Type I · product for the industry's clients

Who did it: Tyler, Blackbaud, Clio, CCC, Verisk, athenahealth, Cerner.

What you have to be. Not one of them was a service provider in general terms. Each was either a participant in the industry — athenahealth owned a chain of clinics; or the holder of a regulatory function — Verisk collects insurer data by law across fifty states; or a buyer of the industry's outsourcing along with its client list — Tyler bought two hundred counties.

An MSP does not own the industry. It serves it from outside and accumulates knowledge of the client's IT environment, not of the client's professional process. These are different assets, and it is the second one that sells.

Type II · product for peers in the trade

Who did it: ConnectWise, Nerdio, IT Glue, BrightGauge, Liongard, LabTech, ScalePad, Gradient.

Forty years of output: two billion-dollar assets — ConnectWise and Nerdio.

An industry observer following the segment since 2008, asked to list the successful transitions, names four people and puts the whole population of such founders at "a few dozen in the world".

The most valuable companies in this market did not come out of MSPs: Datto $6.2bn, NinjaOne $1.9bn, Pax8 $1.7bn, Huntress over $1.5bn.

And a software multiple does not follow from a product revenue mix

Enterprise value to annual revenue; disclosed transactions and quoted prices as of 19 September 2026.

A pure service sold for four times what the product half fetched — inside one company

In 2021 FireEye sold its product business and the name itself for $1.2bn, keeping the services arm. A year later Google bought that services arm for $5.4bn.

The spread inside the product class — 0.53× to 10.1× — is wider than the gap between classes. A service does not condemn a company to a low multiple. The absence of an accumulating asset does.

The entry price paid in services does not wash out, even after twenty-six years

Tyler's gross margin is 43.8% against 70–80% for a software company of that size. Verified directly: gross profit of $936m on revenue of $2,138m.

Gross margin is in general the most reliable indicator of how many live people remain inside "product" revenue: Procore 80% → Veeva 75.5% → Blackbaud 58.8% → Tyler 43.8% → Toast 25.9%.

And two cases widely treated as migrations into software are not: athenahealth at 97.4% services (verified: $1,188.4m of $1,220.3m), Cerner at 77%.

What this changes in the decision. An industry is chosen for the service, not for a future product. An open vendor layer is valuable not as a route into software but as the absence of a competitor already capturing the client's budget. Healthcare and defence contracting qualify because clients there pay for protection under threat of penalty — not because a product will one day grow out of them.
